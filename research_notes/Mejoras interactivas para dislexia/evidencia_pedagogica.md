# Evidence base for teaching early reading to children aged 6-9 with dyslexia (Spanish focus) and implications for digital/interactive activities

Research window: about 15 searches and fetches, October 2026. Several primary PDFs could not be opened (USAID host unreachable, Elsevier paywall), so some figures come from abstracts or secondary evidence maps (ASHA Evidence Maps). Where that applies, the source is named.

## 1. Structured literacy, systematic phonics, Orton-Gillingham (OG) and multisensory approaches: what the meta-analyses say and which components matter

### Takeaway
Explicit, systematic phonics (phoneme-grapheme mapping plus decoding practice) is the only approach with statistically confirmed effects for children with reading disabilities. Those effects are small to moderate (g about 0.3, possibly about 0.2 after correcting for publication bias). Branded OG/multisensory packages do NOT show significant effects beyond comparison instruction. Build the content around phonics components. "Multisensory" is not an active ingredient with evidence of its own.

### Cited Findings
- Galuschka et al. (2014, PLoS ONE) meta-analysed RCTs with children and adolescents who have reading disabilities. Phonics instruction was the most-studied approach and the ONLY one with statistically confirmed efficacy on reading and spelling (g' about 0.32-0.34, small but significant). Other approaches (e.g., reading-fluency training alone, phonemic awareness alone, sunshade/coloured lenses, auditory training, medical treatment) had mean effects that did not reach significance. Interventions longer than 12 weeks tended to show larger effects. — [Galuschka et al. 2014, PLoS ONE PDF](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/33740/1/10.1371_journal.pone.0089900.pdf); [PMC summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8516770)
- A 2021 critique (Toffalini et al., Behavior Research Methods) reports the phonics estimate as g = 0.32, deflated to g about 0.20 after controlling for publication bias. Included trials had a mean of 32 and a median of 20 participants per group, so most were underpowered. — [Toffalini et al. 2021, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-021-01549-x); [preprint PDF](https://research.unipd.it/bitstream/11577/3417421/1/Toffalini%2c%20Giofr%c3%a8%2c%20...%2c%20Szucs%20%282021%29%20-%20Dyslexia%20treatment%20studies.%20Systematic%20review%20suggestions%20small%20effects%20small%20samples.pdf)
- Galuschka, Görgen et al. (2020, Educational Psychologist) meta-analysed spelling interventions for dyslexia. Phonics, morphological and orthographic interventions all had significant effects on reading/spelling outcomes. — [ASHA Evidence Map summary](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/441c03c4-321e-43d6-9bb7-c0e2b6bbfd10)
- Stevens, Austin, Moore, Scammacca, Boucher and Vaughn (2021, Exceptional Children 87(4):397-417) covered 24 studies (16 meta-analysed), K-12, with or at risk of word-level reading disabilities. OG effect on foundational skills was ES = 0.22 (p = .40, not significant). Vocabulary/comprehension was ES = 0.14 (p = .59, not significant). The study corpus was described as low quality with small samples (some n = 10-12). — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8497161); [Reading Rockets](https://readingrockets.org/resources/resource-library/current-state-evidence-examining-effects-orton-gillingham-reading); [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-leading-dyslexia-treatment-isnt-a-magic-bullet-studies-show-while-other-options-show-promise/)
- Contested: Solari, Petscher and Hall (2021) argue the null result reflects weak designs and small samples, and that no studies isolate multisensory exposure. This means "not proven" rather than "proven ineffective". — [Harvard Reach Every Reader](https://reacheveryreader.gse.harvard.edu/what-does-science-say-about-orton-gillingham-interventions/)
- The WWC practice guide "Foundational Skills to Support Reading for Understanding in K-3" (2016) gives these evidence ratings: (1) academic language/vocabulary — minimal; (2) awareness of speech sounds and their link to letters — strong; (3) decoding words, analysing word parts, writing and recognising words — strong; (4) daily reading of connected text for accuracy, fluency and comprehension — moderate. — [WWC guide PDF (Georgia Literacy Center copy)](https://www.galiteracycenter.org/s/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf); [APA Div. 16 summary](https://apadivision16.org/2016/07/new-wwc-practice-guide-strategies-for-teaching-foundational-reading-skills-in-the-early-grades/); [Nebraska DoE chart](https://www.education.ne.gov/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/WWC_Elementary_Guides.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a digital product, the "active ingredient" to build is explicit grapheme-phoneme teaching plus blending, segmenting and decoding of words and pseudowords, plus spelling (encoding). These are WWC recommendations 2-3, rated strong. Multisensory touches (tracing, sound and animation) are fine for engagement, but there is no evidence base to claim them as the mechanism.
- Effects are modest, and longer programmes (12+ weeks) do better. Design for a sustained curriculum, not one-off games.
- Morphological and orthographic (spelling-pattern) activities have meta-analytic support for spelling, so they are worth including, especially from about age 8.

### Gaps
- The National Reading Panel (2000) figures were not re-verified in this session. They are outside the 2010+ window anyway.
- No pooled meta-analysis dedicated to Spanish-language phonics interventions for dyslexia was found.

## 2. Spanish-specific findings: dyslexia as a fluency (speed) deficit, and which interventions help fluency

### Takeaway
In Spanish, dyslexic children quickly reach fairly accurate decoding. The persistent marker is slow, effortful reading. The best-supported fluency tools are repeated reading, especially with a model or listening preview, and training on sub-lexical units (syllables), which Spanish research groups (Defior, Serrano) have built into programmes. Evidence for Spanish programmes is mostly design-level or small-sample.

### Cited Findings
- Serrano and Defior (2008), "Dyslexia speed problems in a transparent orthography", Annals of Dyslexia 58(1):81-95. Its title states the speed-over-accuracy pattern. The full text was not accessed. — [UGR record](https://produccioncientifica.ugr.es/documentos/61b50184ff6ada1aa9a0eebc?lang=en)
- In transparent orthographies such as Spanish, speed problems are more striking than accuracy problems. Difficulty acquiring reading speed is considered the marker of dyslexia, because consistent grapheme-phoneme mappings allow accurate reading even in dyslexic children. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01409/pdf); [Jiménez-Fernández et al., Procedia 2014](https://www.uv.es/femavi/2014_Procedia_Dyslexia.pdf)
- Davies, Cuetos and Glez-Seijas (2007): Spanish dyslexic children read with high accuracy but more slowly than chronological-age controls, and similarly to reading-level controls (delay rather than deviance). A 2013 follow-up splits slowness into speech onset latency vs articulation duration. — [Frontiers 2014 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01409/pdf); [Developmental dyslexia in a transparent orthography (Emerald)](https://emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/S0735-004X(2010)0000023006)
- Gómez-Zapata, Defior and Serrano (2011, Escritos de Psicología 4(2):65-73) designed a Spanish fluency programme combining accelerated reading and repeated reading. It is sequential: syllables, then words, then texts, plus metaphonological training. Rationale: Spanish sub-lexical units (syllables) are easily recognisable, so practising them supports automatisation and faster word recognition. This is a design paper; outcome data are not in the abstract. — [Revista Escritos de Psicología](https://revistas.uma.es/index.php/espsi/article/view/13320)
- A 2025 Spanish-language review (Medicina Buenos Aires) states that explicit, systematic phonological training combined with grapheme-phoneme correspondence is effective short- and long-term. Repeated readings, especially with modelling, increase fluency using sub-lexical, lexical and supra-lexical materials. — [Medicina (B. Aires) 2025 suppl.](https://www.medicinabuenosaires.com/revistas/vol85-25/s1/76s1.pdf)
- Lee and Yoon (2017, J Learning Disabilities 50(2):213-224) meta-analysed 34 repeated-reading (RR) studies (1990-2014, K-12, reading disabilities), with outcome measured in correct words per minute (CWPM). Results:
  - Overall g = 1.41.
  - Elementary g = 1.63 vs secondary g = 0.86.
  - 4+ repeats g = 1.72, vs 3 repeats g = 1.45 and 2 repeats g = 0.82.
  - With listening passage preview (model read first) g = 1.95 vs without g = 0.94.
  - Practised passages g = 1.94 vs transfer passages g = 0.97.
  - No significant difference with or without systematic error correction, goal setting, extrinsic feedback or peer mediation.
  - Caution: heterogeneous single-case and group designs; Chard et al. (2009) judged RR not yet evidence-based. — [ASHA Evidence Map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/c88f557d-4711-4446-a568-49c8bf8c881f); [MetaTOC abstract](https://metatoc.com/papers/62832-the-effects-of-repeated-reading-on-reading-fluency-for-students-with-reading-disabilities-a-meta-analysis)
- Relevant Spanish developmental work: Cuetos and Suárez-Coalla (2009), "From grapheme to word in reading acquisition in Spanish" (Applied Psycholinguistics); Cuetos, Álvarez-Cañizo and Suárez-Coalla (2014) on orthographic learning; Álvarez-Cañizo, Suárez-Coalla and Cuetos (2018) on reading prosody development; Castejón, González-Pumariega and Cuetos (2011) on word-reading fluency acquisition (Infancia y Aprendizaje). — [Redalyc reference list](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/2710/271022095008.pdf); [Uniovi portal](https://portalinvestigacion.uniovi.es/documentos/5e57af012999527d991af9a7?lang=de)
- Rapid naming (RAN) and verbal short-term memory predict reading acquisition alongside phonological awareness. — [Dialnet article citing Badian 2000; Bowers 1995](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/2941251.pdf)

### Inferences
- Core digital activity for Spanish 6-9: timed, repeated reading of short units with a model first. Flow: listen to the passage or word list with highlighting, then read aloud 3-4 times, then plot a CWPM graph. Progress through syllables, then high-frequency syllable chains and words, then phrases and short texts.
- Include transfer texts (new passages with the same syllables), because gains on practised passages are about twice those on transfer passages. Do not mistake in-app gains for generalised fluency.
- Syllable-unit speed drills (e.g., rapid recognition of frequent CV/CVC syllables, building words from syllables) are well motivated for Spanish. Their outcome evidence is weaker than for RR itself.
- Since error correction and goal-setting were not significant moderators in RR, the core is repetition plus a model, not elaborate feedback systems.

### Gaps
- No controlled outcome study of the Gómez-Zapata/Defior/Serrano programme was retrieved.
- I found no study by Suárez-Coalla and Cuetos specifically testing a fluency intervention.
- No direct evidence was found that RAN training improves reading. RAN is a predictor, but trainability is unverified here.
- Evidence on morphological-unit training (prefixes/suffixes as reading units) in Spanish dyslexia was not located in this session.

## 3. Phonological-awareness progression for Spanish, letter introduction order, common confusions

### Takeaway
Use a hierarchy of syllable, then intra-syllabic (onset-rhyme), then phoneme, with phonemic awareness as the highest level. Pair PA with letters early, because in Spanish the alphabetic principle drives reading. Syllable work is easier and can happen in groups; phoneme-level work for struggling children needs more explicit, clinical-type instruction.

### Cited Findings
- Treiman's (1991) hierarchical model (syllabic, intra-syllabic onset/rhyme, phonemic) is the widely accepted framework in Spanish literature. Jiménez and Ortiz (2000) place phonemic awareness at the highest level. — [Dialnet](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/2941251.pdf); [Redalyc](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/1570/157025024001.pdf)
- Syllabic sensitivity develops before phonemic awareness and may predict later reading success. The PA-reading relationship is particularly important in transparent languages like Spanish. — [Onomazein/Psykhe article](https://onomazein.letras.uc.cl/index.php/psykhe/article/download/21101/17441/45697); [Fuentes Humanísticas](https://fuenteshumanisticas.azc.uam.mx/index.php/rfh/article/download/63/62)
- In children with language impairment, classroom group work improved syllable awareness. More clinical intervention was needed to progress in phonemic awareness. — [Hispadoc](https://hispadoc.es/descarga/articulo/3817110.pdf)
- A Chilean programme with preschool and 1st-grade children improved metaphonological skills. — [aacademica](https://www.aacademica.org/000-020/133.pdf)
- Spanish teachers who teach PA in early childhood show mismatches with research-based practice. — [Bordón (RECYT)](https://recyt.fecyt.es/index.php/BORDON/article/download/29038/15496/91764)
- Jiménez and Ortiz (1999): lack of phonological awareness explains part of the difficulties in learning to read and write. — [Redined (Sánchez)](https://redined.educacion.gob.es/xmlui/bitstream/handle/11162/219664/S%c3%a1nchez.pdf?sequence=1)

### Inferences
- Digital PA sequence:
  1. Syllable counting, clapping and segmenting.
  2. Syllable deletion or substitution.
  3. Onset identification (first sound).
  4. Phoneme blending.
  5. Phoneme segmentation.
  6. Phoneme manipulation.
  Link each step to letters as soon as possible. Phonics-linked PA is what the meta-analyses support (Galuschka 2014; WWC Rec. 2).
- Phoneme-level tasks need more scaffolding (audio models, fewer choices, worked examples) than syllable tasks.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed source was retrieved for a standard Spanish letter-introduction sequence (commonly vowels first, then continuant consonants such as m, l, s, p). Treat any sequence as practitioner convention unless further sourced.
- No source was retrieved quantifying common visual or phonological confusions (b/d, p/q, m/n, f/t) in Spanish dyslexic children. Note also that b/v are homophones in Spanish, which creates a spelling-specific confusion. This is linguistic fact; I have no study citation.

## 4. Typography and presentation: fonts, size, spacing, colours and overlays

### Takeaway
Extra letter spacing has the most solid experimental support: immediate gains in accuracy and speed for dyslexic children, including in a transparent orthography. Prefer plain roman sans-serif or monospaced fonts and avoid italics. "Dyslexia fonts" (OpenDyslexic, Dyslexie) show no advantage. Coloured overlays should not be endorsed for dyslexia. These are accessibility supports, not interventions.

### Cited Findings
- Zorzi et al. (2012, PNAS 109(28):11455-11459) tested 74 dyslexic children aged 8-14 (34 Italian, 40 French). Setup: extra-large letter spacing (+2.5 pt between letters, with word and line spacing scaled proportionally). Results:
  - Reading accuracy roughly doubled.
  - Speed rose by more than 20%, or about 0.3 syllables/second after removing order effects (comparable to a year of typical gains in Italian dyslexics).
  - The weakest readers benefited most.
  - There was no difference between transparent (Italian) and opaque (French) orthographies.
  - A follow-up found the benefit only for dyslexics vs reading-level controls.
  - Letter spacing, not line spacing, was critical.
  - Attributed to visual crowding.
  - Limits: one- or two-session lab tests with unrelated sentences; the authors frame it as a supplement to skills training. — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/06/120607105712.htm); [MedicalXpress](https://m.medicalxpress.com/news/2012-06-wider-letter-spacing-dyslexics.html); [Memory-key summary](https://www.memory-key.com/archive/research/news/extra-large-letter-spacing-improves-reading-dyslexia)
- Rello and Baeza-Yates (2013, "Good Fonts for Dyslexia", eye-tracking) tested 48 people with dyslexia aged 11-50. Helvetica, Courier and Arial gave better reading. OpenDyslexic was not better, and participants preferred Arial/Helvetica. Italic hurt (Arial Italic was worst). Sans-serif, monospaced and roman categories were better. A 2016 follow-up (ACM TACCESS) with 97 Spanish speakers (48 with dyslexia) found Helvetica, Courier, Arial and CMU best, italics significantly worse, and sans-serif better than serif. Caveats: adult/teen samples, small n, secondary write-ups disagree on details (Verdana vs CMU; 14 vs 18 pt). — [BYU Editing Research](https://editingresearch.byu.edu/2020/05/28/which-fonts-are-best-for-dyslexia/); [UPF repository](https://repositori.upf.edu/signposting/describedby/e0ca6f40-a0fa-490f-9d03-53d043270deb); [UT DWRL](https://www.dwrl.utexas.edu/?p=1193)
- Coloured overlays and lenses: Griffiths, Taylor, Henderson and Barrett (2016, Ophthalmic & Physiological Optics) reviewed 51 items/54 datasets. They concluded overlays or lenses to ameliorate reading difficulties "cannot be endorsed". Reported benefits are likely placebo, practice or Hawthorne effects. — [White Rose eprints](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/104441/)
- Contested in part: Evans and Allen (2016, J Optometry) found individually prescribed filters can help people with "visual stress", but they are unlikely to affect dyslexia's phonological and memory deficits. Most people with dyslexia do not have visual stress. — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/0c1d9e49b74740cda05af8e9144e28d2); [PMC response letter](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5484782)
- A cohort of 5,822 children found vision problems in dyslexic children rare, and no more common than in controls. — [Medical News Today](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/releases/281202.php)
- Galuschka 2014 also found no significant effect for coloured lenses/overlays as a treatment. — [PLoS ONE PDF](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/33740/1/10.1371_journal.pone.0089900.pdf)

### Inferences
- Default reading settings:
  - Sans-serif roman font with distinct letter shapes (single-storey "a" is common in early-grade fonts).
  - No italics; use bold for emphasis.
  - Generous letter and word spacing, with a user-adjustable spacing slider.
  - Large size.
  - Short lines.
  - Left-aligned text.
- Offer theme and colour preference only as comfort or personalisation. Do not market a "dyslexia colour mode" or "dyslexia font" as therapeutic.

### Gaps
- No pooled evidence was retrieved on optimal font size or line length for children aged 6-9 specifically. The Rello samples are aged 11+.
- Later replications of Zorzi 2012 were not checked in this session. Some later studies reportedly found smaller or speed-only effects (unverified here).

## 5. Text-to-speech (TTS), audio support and read-along highlighting

### Takeaway
TTS/read-aloud gives a moderate boost to COMPREHENSION/access (g about 0.35) by bypassing decoding. It does not train decoding. Audio has its strongest evidence-based role inside fluency practice, as the model "listening passage preview" before the child reads (RR with preview g = 1.95 vs 0.94 without).

### Cited Findings
- Wood, Moxley, Tighe and Wagner (2018, J Learning Disabilities 51(1):73-84) meta-analysed 22 studies of TTS/read-aloud for students with reading disabilities (grade 3 to college). Comprehension effect g = 0.35 (95% CI 0.14-0.56). Study design moderated effects. The authors' conclusion was cautious ("may assist"). — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5494021/); [ASHA Evidence Map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/8f7b9dd3-41fb-48c6-86a5-3e25ad47bc7b)
- TTS works by removing the decoding requirement. — [PMC (Wood et al. author manuscript)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5494021/)
- In repeated reading, a listening passage preview (hearing a model read) doubled effects. — [ASHA Evidence Map, Lee & Yoon 2017](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/c88f557d-4711-4446-a568-49c8bf8c881f)

### Inferences
- Separate the two modes in the product:
  - "Listen to the story" (TTS for access, vocabulary and comprehension).
  - "Practice reading" (child decodes; audio used only as a model before, or as help on demand after an attempt).
- Always-on TTS during decoding practice would likely let the child skip the target skill. This is inferred and not directly tested in the sources retrieved.
- Highlighting the current word or syllable while audio plays is a natural fit with listening preview. No direct effect size was found for highlighting itself.

### Gaps
- Wood et al. starts at grade 3. Evidence for 6-8-year-olds and for Spanish TTS specifically was not found.
- No study isolating synchronised highlighting was retrieved.

## 6. Practice dosage, feedback, errorless learning, motivation and gamification

### Takeaway
Longer interventions (more than 12 weeks) produce larger effects. In RR, more repetitions (4+) help. Game wrappers alone do not improve reading: a trial with phonological and action video games found no reading gain over controls. Gamification should deliver explicit phonics and fluency content, not replace it.

### Cited Findings
- Intervention duration over 12 weeks was associated with larger effect sizes. — [Galuschka et al. 2014](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/33740/1/10.1371_journal.pone.0089900.pdf)
- Repeated reading: 4+ repeats g = 1.72 vs 2 repeats g = 0.82. Systematic error correction, goal setting and extrinsic feedback were not significant moderators. — [ASHA Evidence Map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/c88f557d-4711-4446-a568-49c8bf8c881f)
- Łuniewska et al. (Polish RCT, 54 dyslexic children): action video games and phonological non-action games both improved phonological awareness, but reading progress did not differ from an untrained dyslexic control group. "Neither action nor phonological video games make dyslexic children read better." — [UCC CORA](https://cora.ucc.ie/items/8a31908d-8c7b-42f6-bf1a-cb5fb87ed720)
- A 2023 qualitative meta-synthesis (15 studies) found game-based apps have potential, but noted distraction issues. It pooled no effect sizes. — [CNU Journal of Higher Education](https://jhe.cnu.edu.ph/index.php/ojs3/article/view/233)
- Children with higher pre-training phonological awareness benefited more from a reading game. — [Springer ETR&D 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-019-09658-3)
- GraphoGame in Spanish:
  - A Chilean randomised study (n = 122 first graders, low-SES Araucanía, 4 months) tested it. Outcomes were not available in the snippet.
  - A 2022 pre/post study of 59 Spanish-speaking children (43 with DLD) showed gains in letter recognition, PA and pseudoword recognition after 6 weeks. It had no control group. — [SAGE, Child Language Teaching and Therapy 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/02656590221139232); [UOH repository (Chile)](https://repositorio.uoh.cl/handle/611/566?show=full); [JYX record](https://jyx.jyu.fi/jyx/Record/jyx_123456789_51561)

### Inferences
- Design for short, frequent sessions across a full term or more, and track cumulative exposure. Make the core loop explicit grapheme-phoneme-decoding-fluency content, with game mechanics as packaging.
- Rewards should be tied to practice behaviours (repetitions completed, personal CWPM growth), not to random play. The RR evidence shows extrinsic feedback adds little beyond the repetition itself.

### Gaps
- No source retrieved specifying optimal session length (e.g., 10-20 min) or frequency for 6-9-year-olds with dyslexia.
- No retrieved evidence on spaced or retrieval practice, or errorless learning, specific to dyslexic early readers.
- No quantitative meta-analysis of gamification effects on reading in dyslexia was found.

## 7. Early screening and progress monitoring: Spanish CBM measures and norms

### Takeaway
Words correct per minute (WCPM/PCPM) is the standard CBM metric in Spanish too. Grade explains about 70% of the variance in reading rate. Latin American students read more slowly than students in Spain, so Spain-based or English norms should not be used for El Salvador. A 2018 national EGRA for El Salvador (grades 2-3, IDEL risk benchmarks) exists, but its numbers could not be retrieved in this session.

### Cited Findings
- Ripoll Salceda, Tapia Montesinos and Aguado Alonso (2020, Revista de Psicodidáctica 25:158-165) meta-analysed reading rate in Spanish speakers: 45 publications, 113 oral and 54 silent measures, grade 1 to Bachillerato. Key findings:
  - Grade explains about 70% of variance.
  - CWPM is lower than WPM.
  - Hispanic-American students read more slowly than students in Spain.
  - In early primary, the term of measurement matters.
  - Spanish rates are slower than English estimates.
  Per-grade values are behind a paywall. — [Elsevier](https://www.elsevier.es/en-revista-revista-psicodidactica-243-articulo-reading-rate-in-spanish-speaking-students-S2530380520300046)
- El Salvador EGRA 2018 (RTI for USAID): national sample of public schools, grades 2 and 3, administered late June/early July (end of school year). It includes oral reading fluency, with risk levels classified by IDEL standards (Table 6). — [USAID PDF PA00TFBZ](https://pdf.usaid.gov/pdf_docs/PA00TFBZ.pdf) (the host was unreachable from this environment); [RTI Shared](https://shared.rti.org/node/1037)
- Journalistic benchmark only (Guatemala): about 60 wpm expected at the end of grade 2, and 130-150 in grade 6. No technical source is given, so it is weak. — [Prensa Libre](https://www.prensalibre.com/?p=303835)
- Spanish fluency instruments with norms exist regionally, e.g., Uruguay's Escala de Fluidez basada en Textos (Fumagalli et al., 2022). Norms were not visible. — [UdelaR thesis](https://sifp.psico.edu.uy/sites/default/files/trabajos_finales/archivos/estudiante_virginia_medinatfg_2024_2_3.pdf)
- The CWPM metric combines accuracy and speed. — [Palm Beach Grade 3 Spanish standards](https://www.palmbeachschools.org:443/cms/lib/FL50010848/Centricity/Domain/409/Grade%203%20BEST%20Spanish%20Standards.pdf)

### Inferences
- In-app progress monitoring:
  - A 1-minute oral reading probe (CWPM) on unpractised, grade-level passages, plus accuracy %.
  - Optionally, letter-sound and pseudoword fluency for 6-7-year-olds.
  - Plot growth against the child's own baseline, and against local (Salvadoran or Latin American) benchmarks only once sourced.
- Because in Spanish the deficit is speed, accuracy alone will miss many at-risk children. Monitoring must be timed.

### Gaps
- Exact El Salvador EGRA 2018 CWPM means, IDEL cut-offs and per-grade Spanish norms (Ripoll 2020 tables) were not obtained. Fetch the USAID PDF and the Psicodidáctica full text.
- No source was retrieved on recommended monitoring frequency (e.g., weekly vs biweekly) for Spanish CBM.
